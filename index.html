Module 01

Executive Summary

Briefing

SUBJECT: The Stewardship & Governance Index (SGI) — UC Board of Regents Evaluation

In today's climate of public higher education, governing boards are facing intense challenges at an unprecedented speed. These challenges include structural funding deficits, changing student demographics, and rising external political pressures (Pierce, 2014; Rall et al., 2025). Although governing boards hold ultimate legal authority, they are rarely evaluated using standardized, objective metrics. To address this structural accountability gap, this booklet introduces the Stewardship & Governance Index (SGI)—a robust performance-evaluation framework that translates contemporary academic governance literature into actionable key performance indicators.

Methodological Framework: Drawing on Birnbaum's (1989) cybernetic perspective of academic institutions, the SGI evaluates governing boards across five core dimensions: Institutional Autonomy, Boundary Porosity Integrity, Cybernetic Sensitivity, Fiduciary DEIJ Fluency, and Relational Trust Capital. Within these dimensions, a five-star rubric utilizes observable evidence, including public bylaws, state constitutional mandates, and asset disclosures (Form 700).

Core Findings (Evaluated Institution: UC Board of Regents): Utilizing the SGI, our assessment of the University of California Board of Regents reveals a complex profile. While the Board of Regents scores highly in Boundary Porosity Integrity due to the high connectivity of its corporate networks (Barringer et al., 2022) and strong financial literacy, there is an acute "Representation Gap" regarding its student body. The UC system represents over 290,000 constituents, yet only a single Student Regent represents that entire body. Furthermore, the board exhibits an "Electrical Socket" dynamic (Morgan et al., 2022) regarding diversity, equity, inclusion, and justice (DEIJ) initiatives, showing episodic, crisis-driven intervention rather than continuous, systemic integration.

Strategic Recommendation: To bridge the gap between hierarchical, command-and-control oversight and true shared stewardship, the board must transition from traditional shared governance to a Shared Responsibility framework (Bahls, 2014). This requires expanding student voting seats, establishing rigorous boundary audits, and building continuous, proactive feedback loops with the Academic Senate.

Module 02

Conceptual Glossary

Vocabulary

Fiduciary Stewardship

It’s about who the board is actually looking out for. Legally and ethically, a regent’s job is to protect the university’s long-term legacy and educational mission. It means putting the public trust first, ahead of personal portfolios, corporate ties, or political favors. (UC Regents Bylaw 21; Payette, 2001)

Boundary Porosity

Think of this as how "leaky" or open the university's borders are to the outside world. When a board has high porosity, its trustees act as "boundary spanners." By using their networks to bring in business resources, partnerships and political capital into the institution. (Barringer et al., 2022)

Boundary Porosity Integrity (SGI Metric 2)

The diagnostic metric used to audit what theorists call "boundary network porosity." It measures how successfully a board implements conflict-of-interest checks (such as Form 700 disclosures) to ensure corporate-connected trustees act on behalf of the public good rather than private commercial interests.

The Institutional Umbrella

The conceptual framework where governing boards act as an exterior boundary to shield the institution's academic freedom and autonomy from political, legislative, and external partisan attacks (Rall et al., 2025).

Shared Responsibility

A collaborative effort model where all involved such as trustees, administrators and faculty share accountability of financial and operational initiatives. With the present state working as individual silos into collaborative efforts and involvement across all parties (Bahls, 2014).

Form 700 / Statement of Interests

This is the ultimate "paper trail" for public transparency. In California, it's a legally mandated public document where regents have to disclose their personal investments, real estate holdings, business positions, and income. (California Fair Political Practices Commission)

Module 04

Scorecard Mechanics & Strategic Foundations

Mechanics

To ensure academic and professional balance, the Stewardship & Governance Index (SGI) calculates performance using a qualitative scorecard. Assigning specific weights to key pillars derived from current higher education governance literature:

Evaluation Pillars & Weighting

  • 1. Institutional Autonomy (Umbrella) 25%
  • 2. Boundary Porosity Integrity 20%
  • 3. Cybernetic Sensitivity 20%
  • 4. Fiduciary DEIJ Fluency 20%
  • 5. Relational Trust Capital 15%

Mathematical Formula

SGI = 0.25(A) + 0.20(B) + 0.20(C) + 0.20(D) + 0.15(E)

Final scores are mapped to letter grades:
≥ 4.50: Grade A | 3.50 - 4.49: Grade B | < 3.50: Grade C

Who Applies the SGI?

To maintain absolute objectivity, the SGI is designed to be applied by an independent Joint Stewardship Blue Ribbon Commission. This external body is composed of Academic Senate faculty representatives, student government delegates, public-interest trustees, and third-party higher education governance auditors. This prevents any internal conflict of interest while maintaining academic and financial fluency.

Why the MBA Strategy Lens?

Modern trustees are increasingly drawn from elite corporate backgrounds and struggle to comprehend the decentralized nature of academic shared governance. The MBA Strategy Lens translates academic theories (like cybernetic feedback loops and institutional umbrellas) into the language of corporate risk management and operational KPIs. This bridges the gap, helping corporate-minded trustees align their business acumen with the public trust.

Module 05

Application: UC Board of Regents

Case Study

Why Choose the UC Board of Regents?

The University of California Board of Regents serves as the premier case study for public governance. Managing over 290,000 students and a $40 Billion+ enterprise (UC Annual Financial Report, 2025), its unique constitutional status as a "public trust" (CA Constitution, Art. IX, § 9) places it at the critical intersection of state legislative authority, academic autonomy, and corporate network connectivity. This makes it a critical setting to apply the SGI.

Applying the Stewardship & Governance Index to the UC Board of Regents (Current Assessment):

Pillar 1: Institutional Autonomy (SGI Rating: 3.5 / 5.0)

Evidence: Due to the California Constitution, Sections 9 under Article IX, the UC Regents are held to maintain their status as a "public trust." Evidently, the board is highly sensitive to the state's political administration, even though they are legally shielded from political management. Since 18 of the 26 voting members are directly appointed by the Governor, the board has close proximity to political influence and can be pressured by budget negotiations and campus safety friction. Most of the appointed members of the board have a history of working with the Governor's administration at some point in their careers.

Pillar 2: Boundary Porosity Integrity (SGI Rating: 4.2 / 5.0)

Evidence: In review of the board's public Form 700 disclosures, it reflects that multiple board members hold high and powerful positions within major multinational corporations (e.g., Uber, Disney). While these corporate ties bring valuable expertise to the UC's $40B+ enterprise, they still present risks of structural alignment conflicts. In the case of the UC Regents board, they maintain high compliance with public disclosure but lack structured independent boundary audits.

Pillar 3: Cybernetic Sensitivity (SGI Rating: 3.0 / 5.0)

Evidence: The board interacts with the Academic Senate via Bylaw 40 (which delegates course design and curriculum authority to faculty). But, the board's communication is often reactive rather than proactive. Reactively, the Academic Senate and student councils are consulted after decisions are made, bypassing Birnbaum's (1989) cybernetic feedback loops until a crisis arises.

Pillar 4: Fiduciary DEIJ Fluency (SGI Rating: 2.5 / 5.0)

Evidence: DEI is heavily integrated into formal symbolic plans, but is rarely embedded into standard fiduciary resource allocation or line-item audits. Board members express that the student body is represented across the spectrum, yet the board's own demographic and professional background doesn't reflect this. The available board minutes reveal that the Regents often exhibit the "Electrical Socket" dynamic (Morgan et al., 2022), engaging with student equity challenges episodic and reactive during crises.

Pillar 5: Relational Trust Capital (SGI Rating: 2.8 / 5.0)

Evidence: There is a distinct "Representational Gap" on the board. With only one voting student regent representing over 290,000 students, the board's relational trust capital feels low. Activism and public comment are the primary channels for student feedback, often occurring too far into a problem and leading to reactivity. This signals a breakdown in relational, trust-based leadership (Kezar, 2004).

Weighted SGI Score 3.21 / 5.0
1. Institutional Autonomy (25% Weight) 3.5
What is Checked:

Shielding policies, defense of academic freedom, and structural resistance to gubernatorial or partisan political agendas.

Why Weighted 25%:

Autonomy is the baseline. As the 'Institutional Umbrella' (Rall et al., 2025), if a board cannot shield scholars from state interference, other governance pillars fail.

2. Boundary Porosity Integrity (20% Weight) 4.2
What is Checked:

Form 700 declarations, external corporate network overlaps, and systemic checks against private/market-driven interests.

Why Weighted 20%:

Elite boards are highly porous (Barringer et al., 2022). Unmitigated corporate conflicts threaten the fiduciary commitment to higher education as a public trust.

3. Cybernetic Sensitivity (20% Weight) 3.0
What is Checked:

Latency in responding to Faculty Senate actions, adherence to Bylaw 40, and the presence of non-crisis administrative feedback channels.

Why Weighted 20%:

Cybernetic self-correction (Birnbaum, 1989) is key to campus health. Slower stakeholder feedback acts as a strategic thermostat to prevent institutional crisis.

4. Fiduciary DEIJ Fluency (20% Weight) 2.5
What is Checked:

Alignment of diversity goals with line-item resource allocation, capital budgets, and regular performance evaluations.

Why Weighted 20%:

This counteracts the 'Electrical Socket' dynamic (Morgan et al., 2022) by ensuring equity remains a steady, active commitment instead of a reactive public PR tool.

5. Relational Trust Capital (15% Weight) 2.8
What is Checked:

The Representation Gap, the structure of student/staff advisory roles, and proactive non-crisis engagements with the campus.

Why Weighted 15%:

Trust is the 'social glue' of governance (Kezar, 2004). Strong relationships ensure that hard decisions can be made without causing structural division.
