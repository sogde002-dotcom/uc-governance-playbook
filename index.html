Module 01

1-Page Executive Summary

Briefing

SUBJECT: The Stewardship & Governance Index (SGI) — UC Board of Regents Evaluation

In today's climate public higher education, governing boards are facing intense challenges at an unprecedented speed. Those challenges include structural funding deficits, changing student demographics, and rising external political pressures (Pierce, 2014; Rall et al., 2025). Regardless of the governing boards holding all legal authority, boards are rarely evaluated using standardized metrics. Within this summary, there will be the introduction of the Stewardship & Governance Index (SGI), which is a performance-evaluation framework that captures latest academic governance literature into key performance indicators.

Methodological Framework: In Birnbaum, 1989 the idea of a cybernetic perspective of academic institutions showcases the SGI evaluation of boards across five core dimensions. Those being Institutional Autonomy, Boundary Network Integrity, Cybernetic Sensitivity, Fiduciary DEIJ Fluency, and Relational Trust Capital. Within these dimensions the five star rubric utilizes observable evidence like public bylaws, state constitutional mandates and asset disclosures (Form 700).

Core Findings (Evaluated Institute: UC Board of Regents): Within the core findings using the SGI of the University of California Board of Regents has a complex profile. While the Board of Regents scores highly in the Boundary Network Integrity there is a large gap in the "Representation Gap" of its student body. The board scored high in the Boundary Network Integrity due to its high connectivity of its corporate networks (Barringer et al., 2022) and strong financial literacy. Contrastly, the board is composed of 26 voting seats, but only one of those voting members is a student. Shockingly enough, the UC system represents 290,000 constituents and only one student represents that entire body. Furthermore, the board exhibits an "Electrical Socket" dynamic (Morgan et al., 2022) regarding DEIJ initiatives, showing episodic, crisis-driven intervention rather than continuous, systemic integration.

Strategic Recommendation: To bridge the gap between the command of control to a true shared stewardship, the board must transition from a traditional shared governance to shared responsibility framework. (Bahls, 2014). This looks like increasing the student voting seats, establishing rigourous boundary audits, and building continuous proactive feedback loops within the Academic Senate.

Module 02

Conceptual Glossary

Vocabulary

Fiduciary Stewardship

The legal and ethical obligation of board members to prioritize the institution’s long-term legacy and educational mission over personal, corporate, or political interests (Regents Bylaw 21; Payette, 2001).

Boundary Porosity

The degree to which institutional borders are open to external environments. High porosity allows trustees to act as "boundary spanners," connecting the university to corporate networks and resources (Barringer et al., 2022).

Cybernetic Feedback Loops

Self-correcting mechanisms (like an institutional "thermostat") that monitor changes from acceptable levels of functioning and activate forces to return the institution to a stable state (Birnbaum, 1989).

The Institutional Umbrella

The conceptual framework wherein governing boards act as a protective barrier to shield the institution's academic freedom and autonomy from external political and legislative attacks (Rall et al., 2025).

Shared Responsibility

A collaborative governance model where trustees, administrators, and faculty share key financial and operational realities to co-create strategic initiatives, shifting away from adversarial silos (Bahls, 2014).

Form 700 / Statement of Interests

A public financial disclosure mandated by the California Fair Political Practices Commission (FPPC). It requires public officials to report assets, income, and business positions to detect potential conflicts of interest.

Module 04

Scorecard Methodology

Mechanics

To ensure academic and professional balance, the Stewardship & Governance Index (SGI) calculates performance using a qualitative scorecard. Assigning specific weights to key pillars derived from current higher education governance literature:

Evaluation Pillars & Weighting

  • 1. Institutional Autonomy (Umbrella) 25%
  • 2. Boundary Network Integrity 20%
  • 3. Cybernetic Sensitivity 20%
  • 4. Fiduciary DEIJ Fluency 20%
  • 5. Relational Trust Capital 15%

Mathematical Formula

SGI = 0.25(A) + 0.20(B) + 0.20(C) + 0.20(D) + 0.15(E)

Final scores are mapped to letter grades:
≥ 4.50: Grade A | 3.50 - 4.49: Grade B | < 3.50: Grade C

Measurement Rubric Scale (1.0 - 5.0)

5.0 Star — Exceptional

Significant and accessible evidence of written policies, public records of consistent alignment, transparency, and actionable shielding of campus autonomy.

3.0 Star — Meets Standard

Formal structures exist (e.g., bylaws, open sessions) but the board remains passive, engaging with stakeholders primarily during crises.

1.0 Star — Deficient

Significant disconnect, regular circumvention of standard feedback systems, and unmitigated conflicts of interest.

Module 05

Example Application: UC Board of Regents

Case Study

Applying the Stewardship & Governance Index to the UC Board of Regents (Current Assessment):

Pillar 1: Institutional Autonomy (SGI Rating: 3.5 / 5.0)

Evidence: Evaluating the board's public Form 700 disclosures shows that multiple appointed regents sit on the boards of major multinational corporations (e.g., Uber, Disney). While these corporate ties bring valuable connections to UC's $40B+ enterprise, they present risks within its integratity. Currently the board maintains high compliance with public disclosure, but lacks consistent audits and accountability.

Pillar 2: Boundary Network Integrity (SGI Rating: 4.2 / 5.0)

Evidence: Evaluating the board's public Form 700 disclosures shows that multiple appointed regents sit on the boards of major multinational corporations (e.g., Uber, Disney). While these corporate ties bring valuable expertise to UC's $40B+ enterprise, they present risks of structural alignment conflicts. The board maintains high compliance with public disclosure, but lacks structured audits.

Pillar 3: Cybernetic Sensitivity (SGI Rating: 3.0 / 5.0)

Evidence: The board interacts with the Academic Senate via Bylaw 40 (which delegates course design and curriculum authority to faculty). But, the board's communication is often reactive rather than proactive. Reactivity the Academic Senate and student councils are consulted after decisions are made bypassing Birnbaum's (1989) cybernetic feedback loops until a crisis arises.

Pillar 4: Fiduciary DEIJ Fluency (SGI Rating: 2.5 / 5.0)

Evidence: DEI is heavily integrated into formal symbolic plans, but is rarely embedded into standard fiduciary resource allocation or line-item audits. Examples of this show that Board members express that the student body is across the spectrum represented yet the board doesn't exhibit this. The available board minutes reveals that the Regents often exhibit the "Electrical Socket" dynamic (Morgan et al., 2022) as well.

Pillar 5: Relational Trust Capital (SGI Rating: 2.8 / 5.0)

Evidence: There is a distinct "Representational Gap" on the board. With only one voting student regent representing over 290,000 students, the board's relational trust capital feels low. Activism and public comment are the primary channels for student feedback often to far into the problem leading to reactivity. Which signals a breakdown in relational, trust-based leadership (Kezar, 2004).
